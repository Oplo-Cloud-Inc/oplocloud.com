# OEdu Learn-by-Doing Benchmark

The vision, not the current state. This is the bar every lesson, unit and course is measured against. A course that reads well but scores low here is not finished.

## The one idea

**A student learns a thing by doing the thing, getting a real result back, and adjusting.** Text and video exist only to unblock the next action. They are never the lesson.

Test in one line: *take away every paragraph of explanation. Can the student still learn from what is left?* If yes, the course is doing its job. If the lesson collapses, it was reading.

## Hard gates (any one fails the lesson, no score)

1. **Reading-first.** The student meets more than ~60 words of prose before their first action.
2. **Passive completion.** A step can be completed by scrolling, clicking "Next", or watching. Something the student *did* must be what moves them on (nothing is locked, but nothing is *counted* as done until the doing happens).
3. **Answer-recognition only.** The main check is multiple choice on a fact just read. Recognition is a warm-up, not proof.
4. **No feedback loop.** The student acts and the system shows nothing back (no result, no consequence, no change in the world).
5. **Dead end on error.** A wrong attempt produces only "Incorrect." with no way to see why or try again immediately.

## The six dimensions (score each 0–3)

| # | Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Doing ratio**: share of time spent acting vs. reading/watching | under 30% | 30–50% | 50–75% | over 75% |
| 2 | **Real consequence**: does the action change something visible, live, and true to the subject? | nothing changes | canned animation | reacts to input | a working model of the subject (simulator, real tool, real data) that behaves as the real thing does |
| 3 | **Productive failure**: is attempting-before-being-told the default? | told first, then quizzed | hint on demand | try first, explanation after | try first, failure is informative, student can inspect *why* and retry instantly |
| 4 | **Transfer**: does the student use the skill in a new situation, not the one taught? | same example repeated | tweaked numbers | new scenario | open problem with no template, judged on outcome |
| 5 | **Artifact**: does the student leave with something they made? | none | a score | a filled worksheet | a real thing: a model, a plan, a program, a lab result, a written argument, a portfolio piece |
| 6 | **Evidence of mastery**: how do we know they can do it? | seat time | quiz score | performance on a task | performance on a fresh task, later, without hints |

**Lesson grade** = sum out of 18.

- **15–18 Benchmark.** This is OEdu.
- **11–14 Close.** Ship, then fix the lowest dimension.
- **7–10 Textbook with buttons.** Rework before it goes live.
- **0–6 A reading assignment.** Rebuild.

## The learning loop every lesson should run

1. **Situation.** One sentence of context. A goal the student cares about.
2. **Attempt.** The student does something with no instruction beyond the goal.
3. **Consequence.** The world responds: it works, breaks, or surprises.
4. **Insight.** One short line, shown *after*, naming what just happened.
5. **Variation.** Change a condition; the student predicts, then tests the prediction.
6. **Use.** A new situation where the skill must be applied unaided.

A lesson that skips steps 2–3 is a lecture. A lesson that skips 5–6 is a demo.

## Subject-by-subject: what "doing" means

| Subject | Reading version (fails) | Doing version (passes) |
|---|---|---|
| Math / SAT | worked example, then 10 problems | manipulate the object (graph, figure, expression) and watch the answer emerge; build the strategy from failed attempts |
| Geometry | theorem statement and proof | construct it, drag it, break it, discover the invariant, then prove what you saw |
| Science / Bio | diagram and vocabulary | run the experiment, change a variable, read real data, explain the result |
| History | chapter with dates | sources in hand: make the call a historian or decision-maker had to make, then see what happened |
| Business | case study to read | run the business: set prices, hire, get the cash-flow consequence, recover |
| Coding | reading syntax | write it, run it, read the failure, fix it (the editor *is* the lesson) |
| Finance / EFM | explanation of accounting | post entries into real books and see the statements move |

## Pacing and friction

- **First action within 20 seconds.** The student should be doing before they have decided whether to care.
- **Instant feedback (under 1 second)** for anything the student manipulates.
- **Explanation is pulled, not pushed.** Hints, worked examples and theory sit behind "I'm stuck" and appear after attempts.
- **Nothing is locked.** Students may jump to any step. The benchmark measures doing, so skipping is allowed and simply leaves the step uncounted.
- **Short loops.** Attempt-to-consequence cycle under 2 minutes; a lesson holds many loops, not one long one.

## Measurable signals (instrument these)

| Signal | Target |
|---|---|
| Time to first student action | < 20 s |
| Active-time share (input events vs. idle/scroll) | > 70% |
| Words shown before first action | < 60 |
| Attempts before explanation is opened | ≥ 1 on 80% of steps |
| Retry rate after a wrong attempt | > 85% retry immediately |
| Fresh-task success 7 days later, no hints | > 70% |
| Lessons ending in a student-made artifact | 100% |

## How to run the benchmark

1. **Strip test.** Hide all prose. Does anything teachable remain?
2. **Blank-student test.** Watch a real student with no prior knowledge. Note the first minute of confusion; that is where the lesson needs a better first action, not more text.
3. **Skip test.** Jump to the last step cold. A doing-based lesson should let a capable student succeed and skip the rest.
4. **Break test.** Try to get every wrong answer. Each should teach something.
5. **Week-later test.** Same skill, new problem, no hints.
6. Score the six dimensions; record grade and lowest dimension in the course file.

## Where the vision is aimed

- A student who can *do* the thing on day one, then learns why.
- Teachers who see what students *did*, not what they clicked.
- Courses judged by what students can build, run, fix and defend, not by pages completed.
